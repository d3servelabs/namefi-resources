The **.ca domain** is the official [country-code top-level domain](/en/glossary/cctld/) (ccTLD) for **Canada**, and one of the oldest ccTLDs on the internet — delegated in 1987, years before the modern commercial web existed. Unlike an open, marketed ccTLD, .ca is deliberately gated: only registrants with a genuine connection to Canada, defined precisely by the **Canadian Presence Requirements**, can hold one. For a Canadian business, non-profit, or individual, .ca is the clearest way to signal "this is Canadian" in a web address.

This page covers what .ca is, who is eligible to register one, its history, and the honest trade-offs on pricing, SEO, and reputation.

## What is .ca?

**.ca** is the [ccTLD](/en/glossary/cctld/) assigned to Canada under the ISO 3166-1 two-letter country-code system that [IANA](https://www.iana.org/domains/root/db/ca.html) uses to delegate national domains. Unlike a generic top-level domain such as [.com](/en/tld/com/), .ca has no [ICANN](/en/glossary/icann/) Registry Agreement at all — governing rules come from the national registry itself.

That registry is the **Canadian Internet Registration Authority (CIRA)**, a member-based non-profit that IANA lists as the ccTLD manager. Because .ca genuinely functions as a national namespace, Google treats it as country-targeted rather than generic: it is absent from [Google Search Central's short list of ccTLDs treated as generic](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/managing-multi-regional-sites?hl=en), a list that includes vanity extensions like [.tv](/en/tld/tv/) and [.me](/en/tld/me/). A .ca site sends a genuine "this is for Canada" signal — an asset for Canada-focused projects, a constraint for anyone chasing a global audience.

## History of .ca

- **1987 — Volunteer beginnings.** IANA allocated .ca to **John Demco**, a University of British Columbia systems administrator, on May 14, 1987. Demco and a small group of volunteers ran the registry informally for over a decade, growing it to roughly 100,000 registrations by 2000.
- **1998–2000 — CIRA takes shape.** The Canadian Domain Name Consultative Committee recommended a non-profit successor, and CIRA was incorporated in December 1998. Following an [IANA redelegation report](https://www.iana.org/reports/2000/ca-report-01dec00.html), the .ca registry was formally redelegated to CIRA effective December 1, 2000.
- **2000 — Canadian Presence Requirements introduced.** Alongside the CIRA handover, the modern eligibility framework — the Canadian Presence Requirements — took effect on November 8, 2000, replacing the old, more improvised rules.
- **2008–2020 — Growth milestones.** .ca crossed 1 million registrations in 2008 and 3 million in 2020, according to CIRA's own history.
- **2012 — French-character IDN launch.** To mark .ca's 25th anniversary, CIRA launched support for domain names containing French accented characters, a deliberate nod to Canada's bilingual character.
- **2013 — DNSSEC.** CIRA published its first cryptographically signed .ca zone in January 2013, following a key-signing ceremony the previous September.

## How people use .ca

- **Canadian small and mid-size businesses** that want their nationality legible in the domain itself.
- **Federal, provincial, and municipal-adjacent organizations** and non-profits, where a .ca suffix reinforces local trust.
- **Canadian tech startups and agencies** — Toronto, Vancouver, and Montreal have active .ca-first developer and startup communities.
- **Bilingual (English/French) projects** taking advantage of accented-character IDN support.
- **Domain hacks and short brandables**, where the shrinking pool of available .com names pushes builders toward .ca.

**Who it's not ideal for:** anyone without a genuine Canadian tie — the Canadian Presence Requirements make .ca unregistrable for most applicants outside Canada — and global brands whose primary audience is not Canadian, since the ccTLD's geo-targeting works against a worldwide ranking strategy.

## Notable sites using .ca

.ca is used heavily by Canadian institutions for their primary or Canada-specific web presence — national broadcasters, provincial governments, and universities commonly hold and actively use .ca addresses alongside or instead of .com. Because individual organizational sites change branding over time, the more durable, verifiable fact is structural: CIRA's own reporting puts total .ca registrations at roughly 3 million-plus, one of the largest ccTLD namespaces in the world by volume, dominated by real Canadian organizations rather than parked or speculative names.

## Why choose .ca?

- **Instant Canadian credibility** — the suffix itself signals local presence to Canadian customers, regulators, and partners.
- **Restricted namespace, cleaner neighborhood** — because eligibility is gated, .ca carries less of the bulk-registration spam pattern seen in wide-open new gTLDs.
- **Bilingual-ready** — native support for French accented characters fits Canada's official bilingualism.
- **Long track record** — one of the internet's original ccTLDs, in continuous operation since 1987.

## Things to consider

- **Eligibility gate.** You must fit a defined Canadian Presence Requirements category; foreign companies without a qualifying Canadian tie generally cannot register directly.
- **Geo-targeted, not global.** The same restriction that builds trust in Canada works against ranking for international, non-Canada-specific search intent.
- **Verification friction.** CIRA can request proof of Canadian presence (Registrant Information Validation), which can delay a registration if documentation isn't ready.
- **Smaller namespace than .com.** Fewer registrants means less brand recognition outside Canada.

## Who can register a .ca domain?

**Registration restrictions: gated by the Canadian Presence Requirements (CPR).** Every registrant must select one of 18 defined categories under CIRA's official [Canadian Presence Requirements for Registrants](https://www.cira.ca/uploads/2019/04/canadian-presence-requirements-for-registrants.pdf). The main categories include:

- **Canadian citizens** of the age of majority.
- **Permanent residents** who are "ordinarily resident in Canada" — defined as residing in Canada more than 183 days in the twelve months before applying, and in every twelve-month period thereafter.
- **Canadian-incorporated businesses**, partnerships, trusts, and associations meeting Canadian-ownership thresholds.
- **Educational institutions, libraries, hospitals, Indigenous governing bodies, and government entities** located in Canada.
- **Trademark holders** — a narrower allowance for owners of a trademark registered under Canada's *Trade-marks Act*, limited to the exact word component of that mark, even without otherwise meeting the CPR.

Foreign companies without one of these qualifying ties generally cannot register a .ca name directly. IDN registrations (French accented characters) are supported, [DNSSEC](/en/glossary/dnssec) has been available since 2013, and CIRA offers WHOIS privacy protection for individual registrants by default. The authoritative rules live in CIRA's [Canadian Presence Requirements for Registrants](https://www.cira.ca/uploads/2019/04/canadian-presence-requirements-for-registrants.pdf) policy document.

## .ca pricing and value

.ca pricing dynamics follow the same shape as most gated ccTLDs: **first-year and renewal pricing can differ**, with promotional first-year rates common while the standing renewal rate applies long term — budget for the renewal, not the introductory offer. Because .ca is restricted rather than open, it lacks the large premium-name marketplace of unrestricted gTLDs; cost is driven mainly by registrar margin and any registrar-specific premium classification for short or high-demand strings. We list no figures here; check current rates at the point of purchase.

## Reputation and email deliverability

.ca carries a solid reputation. Because registration requires meeting the Canadian Presence Requirements, the namespace has not attracted the bulk-registration spam patterns documented on some wide-open new gTLDs, and mail providers generally treat it no differently from other established ccTLDs. Proper SPF, DKIM, and DMARC configuration still matters, but .ca has no documented history of the abuse-list flagging seen on some low-cost, unrestricted extensions.

## Branding and naming tips

- **Lean on the "Canadian" signal.** .ca reads instantly as Canadian — use that instead of spelling out "Canada" in the name itself.
- **Consider French-accented names if bilingual.** IDN support lets you register names with é, à, ç, and similar characters.
- **Keep it short.** With a restricted registrant pool, short and clean .ca names are more attainable than the equivalent [.com](/en/tld/com/).
- **Don't rely on it for a global audience.** If most traffic is outside Canada, .ca's geo-targeting works against you.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can anyone register a .ca domain?

No. .ca is restricted under CIRA's Canadian Presence Requirements. Eligible registrants include Canadian citizens, permanent residents, Canadian-incorporated businesses and organizations, and a narrower category for owners of a registered Canadian trademark. There is no open, anyone-anywhere registration.

### Does a .ca domain affect SEO?

Yes. Google treats .ca as a country-code TLD geo-targeted to Canada, not a generic domain, since it does not appear on Google's short list of ccTLDs treated as generic. That helps Canadian-focused sites rank for Canadian searches and works against sites chasing a global audience.

### Who runs the .ca domain?

The Canadian Internet Registration Authority (CIRA), a member-based non-profit, has managed .ca since it took over the registry from volunteer administrator John Demco in December 2000. CIRA is listed as the ccTLD manager in the IANA root-zone database.

### What are the Canadian Presence Requirements?

The Canadian Presence Requirements (CPR) are CIRA's eligibility rules for .ca. Registrants must fit one of about 18 defined categories connected to Canada, from citizenship and 183-day residency to incorporation under Canadian law, with a limited allowance for holders of an exact-match registered Canadian trademark.
