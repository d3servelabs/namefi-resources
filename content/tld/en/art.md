The **.art domain** is a generic top-level domain built for the global creative community — artists, galleries, museums, art fairs, and increasingly NFT and digital-art platforms. Operated by **UK Creative Ideas Limited**, it is one of the more successful "vertical" new gTLDs, with real adoption from institutions like the Louvre and Tate rather than just speculative registrations. This page covers where .art came from, who genuinely uses it, and the honest trade-offs of choosing it.

## What is .art?

.art is a [generic top-level domain](/en/glossary/tld) (gTLD), not a country-code extension, so it carries no geographic association. Its registry, **UK Creative Ideas Limited**, entered into a **Base, Non-Sponsored** registry agreement with ICANN on **24 March 2016** — you can confirm the operator and agreement details on the [ICANN Registry Agreement for .art](https://www.icann.org/en/registry-agreements/details/art) and the delegation record on the [IANA root-zone entry for .art](https://www.iana.org/domains/root/db/art.html), which lists a registration date of 2016-06-09 and delegation date of 2016-06-22.

Because .art is generic rather than geographic, [Google treats any TLD resolving through the IANA DNS root zone as a gTLD unless ICANN specifically lists it as a ccTLD](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/managing-multi-regional-sites), so a .art site is not geo-targeted to any single country by default.

## History of .art

.art has an unusually well-documented origin story for a new gTLD:

- **A contested application (2012–2016).** The .art string was one of the more competitive rounds in ICANN's New gTLD Program. Per [Wikipedia's history of the .art TLD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/.art_(top-level_domain)), London-based investor and art collector **Ulvi Kasimov** won the contract for UK Creative Ideas Limited after outbidding nine other applicants and investing roughly $25 million — unusually large for a niche new gTLD.
- **Launch (2016–2017).** The registry agreement was signed 24 March 2016; the string went through a Qualified Launch Program and Limited Registration Period before reaching **general availability on 10 May 2017**.
- **Early institutional adoption.** Museums and galleries were unusually fast to adopt the extension relative to other new gTLDs — the Louvre, Tate, and other cultural institutions registered .art domains within the first year or two of launch, a pattern most new gTLDs never achieve.
- **"Digital Twin" patent (2020).** Per the same Wikipedia entry, the registry received a US patent for a "Digital Twin" service that stores structured artwork metadata (using Object ID standard fields) inside WHOIS-adjacent records — an unusual technical feature tying the domain layer directly to art provenance data.

## How people use .art

Real, specific niches where .art shows up:

- **Museums and cultural institutions** — official or redirect domains for major galleries and museums.
- **Individual artists and photographers** — portfolio sites at `firstname.art` or a chosen artist name.
- **Galleries and art dealers** — commercial gallery sites and online viewing rooms.
- **NFT and digital-art platforms** — projects at the intersection of art and [Web3](/en/glossary/web3/) using the extension for on-the-nose branding.
- **Art fairs, schools, and nonprofits** — event and institutional sites that want an immediate creative signal.

**Who it's not ideal for:** general-purpose businesses outside the art and creative sector, and anyone who needs the broadest possible mainstream recognition — .com or a sector-specific gTLD still reads as more neutral to non-art audiences.

## Notable sites using .art

Unlike many niche new gTLDs, .art has real, verifiable adoption by recognizable institutions:

- **louvre.art** — redirects to the official Musée du Louvre website (louvre.fr).
- **tate.art** — redirects to Tate's official art collection pages (tate.org.uk/art).
- **async.art** — a live NFT art platform (Async Art) operating directly on the extension, illustrating the Web3/digital-art use case.

The registry's own site also cites early adoption by other museums (Guggenheim, ICA London, Centre Pompidou) and brands including Louis Vuitton and Bank of America, per [UK Creative Ideas Limited](https://www.art.art/about).

## Why choose .art?

- **Genuine sector fit.** Few new gTLDs have real institutional buy-in; .art has verifiable museum and gallery adoption rather than just speculative registrations.
- **Open and unrestricted.** No credentials or membership required — any artist, hobbyist, or organization can register.
- **Community programs.** Partnerships like the [American Alliance of Museums](https://www.aam-us.org/member-benefit-claim-a-complimentary-art-domain/) complimentary-domain program and the registry's Art Therapy initiative give .art a genuine community layer most gTLDs lack.
- **Availability.** Short, meaningful names are still attainable in .art where the equivalent .com is long gone.

## Things to consider

- **Narrower general recognition.** Outside art-world circles, .art is less instantly familiar than .com, so a general-audience business may confuse rather than clarify.
- **Premium pricing on desirable strings.** Because the registry actively markets to museums and brands, short or dictionary-word names can carry premium registration fees.
- **Sector mismatch risk.** A name that reads well in .art can read oddly if your actual business (e.g., art *supplies* retail, or an unrelated tech product) isn't really about art itself.
- **Newer than legacy extensions.** As with any new gTLD, some users still default to trusting .com first on sight.

## Who can register a .art domain?

**Registration restrictions: open to all.** .art is an unrestricted generic TLD — there is no credential, professional-membership, or local-presence requirement. Any individual or organization worldwide can register through an accredited [registrar](/en/glossary/registrar).

Trademark holders had a sunrise period (7 December 2016 – 7 February 2017) and a subsequent trademark claims period before general availability opened on 10 May 2017, so the land-rush phase is long over — registration today is first-come, first-served. The extension supports internationalized domain names (IDNs) and [DNSSEC](/en/glossary/dnssec), and [WHOIS privacy](/en/glossary/whois-privacy/) is available through most registrars. Standard ICANN lifecycle rules apply — including auto-renew, redemption-grace, and pending-delete periods. The binding contractual rules are set out in the [ICANN Registry Agreement for .art](https://www.icann.org/en/registry-agreements/details/art).

## .art pricing and value

.art pricing dynamics follow the standard new-gTLD pattern rather than any single figure: **first-year and renewal pricing typically differ**, so budget for the ongoing renewal rate, not an introductory offer. Because the registry actively courts institutions and brands, it also reserves a set of **premium names** — short, dictionary, or high-demand strings — that carry higher registration and often higher renewal costs. The AAM museum partnership and similar community programs can offset first-year cost for eligible members. Length, desirability, premium classification, and registrar margin are the main cost drivers; we list no figures here — check current rates at the point of purchase.

## Reputation and email deliverability

.art does not carry the spam-abuse reputation affecting some ultra-low-cost new gTLDs. Its registrant base skews toward museums, galleries, artists, and brands rather than bulk, disposable registrations, and the registry's institutional partnerships (AAM, Art Therapy Initiative) work against the anonymous mass-registration pattern that draws abuse scrutiny elsewhere. Some conservative spam filters still weight unfamiliar extensions more cautiously than .com — standard **SPF, DKIM, and DMARC** configuration remains good practice for any domain used for email.

## Branding and naming tips

- **Let the suffix do double duty.** `.art` reads naturally after a name, medium, or style — `jane.art`, `modern.art`, `studio.art` — so the domain itself can carry meaning rather than just branding.
- **Consider the Object ID / provenance angle.** For galleries and dealers, the registry's metadata-linking features (per its "Digital Twin" work) can be a genuine differentiator to mention in your pitch, not just a domain choice.
- **Avoid generic art-adjacent words if you're not actually in the art sector.** The suffix's strength is specificity — using it for an unrelated business dilutes the signal for everyone.
- **Keep it short and legible.** Short names are still available in .art where .com equivalents are exhausted — take advantage of that while it lasts.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can anyone register a .art domain?

Yes. .art is an open, unrestricted generic top-level domain operated by UK Creative Ideas Limited. There is no requirement to prove you are a professional artist or art institution — any individual or organization worldwide can register through an accredited registrar.

### Does a .art domain affect SEO?

No. Google treats .art as a generic top-level domain with no geographic bias and no built-in ranking boost or penalty. Rankings depend on the usual content, authority, and technical SEO factors, not the extension itself.

### Who should register a .art domain?

Artists, galleries, museums, art schools, art fairs, NFT and digital-art platforms, photographers, designers, and collectors who want a domain that immediately signals a creative or art-market focus rather than a generic business identity.

### Do museums and galleries actually use .art domains?

Yes. Major institutions including the Louvre and Tate have registered .art domains that redirect to their official sites, and the American Alliance of Museums runs a partnership offering members a complimentary first-year .art domain.
