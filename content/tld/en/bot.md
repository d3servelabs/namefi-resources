The **.bot domain** is a generic top-level domain that says exactly what it is: a namespace for bots, chatbots, and — increasingly — AI agents. It is operated by **Amazon Registry Services, Inc.**, and has an unusual history: it launched restricted to verified bot operators, spent roughly six years as a niche, gated extension, and only opened to unrestricted public registration in October 2023. This page covers that history, the current registration rules, and whether it fits your project.

## What is .bot?

.bot is a [generic top-level domain](/en/glossary/tld) (gTLD), not a country-code extension, so it carries no geographic association. Its registry operator is **Amazon Registry Services, Inc.**, under a **Base, Non-Sponsored** registry agreement with ICANN signed **18 December 2014** — confirmed on the [ICANN Registry Agreement for .bot](https://www.icann.org/en/registry-agreements/details/bot). The [IANA root-zone entry for .bot](https://www.iana.org/domains/root/db/bot.html) lists a registration date of 2015-11-12 and delegation date of 2015-12-02, with Nominet UK handling back-end technical operations.

Because it is generic rather than geographic, [Google treats any TLD resolving through the IANA DNS root zone as a gTLD unless ICANN lists it as a ccTLD](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/managing-multi-regional-sites), so a .bot site carries no automatic geo-targeting.

## History of .bot

.bot has one of the more dramatic eligibility U-turns among new gTLDs:

- **Restricted launch (2017–2023).** Per ICANN's [.bot sunrise and claims period record](https://newgtlds.icann.org/en/program-status/sunrise-claims-periods/bot), .bot ran a sunrise period from 31 May to 14 July 2017, followed by a **Limited Registration Period lasting from 5 December 2017 to 23 February 2023** — over five years — during which Amazon required registrants to prove they operated a functioning bot or chatbot before a name would be allocated.
- **The gate comes down (October 2023).** As reported by [Domain Name Wire](https://domainnamewire.com/2023/11/01/amazon-makes-bot-domain-names-easier-to-register/), "you had to have a bot to register the domains... [in October 2023] Amazon belatedly removed the restrictions for .bot. Now, anyone can register one." The same report notes the .bot zone grew from roughly 1,300 to over 2,000 registrations within the first day of unrestricted registration.
- **Current policy confirms open allocation.** The registry's own [.bot Registration Policy (last updated June 20, 2024)](https://m.media-amazon.com/images/G/01/arsi/documents/2024.06.20_.BOT_GA_Registration_Policy_-_FINAL.pdf) states plainly under "Allocation": *"We will allocate .BOT Domain Names on a first-come, first-served basis"* — with no eligibility-verification clause anywhere in the document.
- **AI-era relevance.** The 2023 opening coincided with the boom in consumer-facing chatbots and AI agents, giving the extension fresh relevance beyond its original narrow developer-bot framing.

## How people use .bot

Real, specific niches where .bot shows up:

- **Chatbot and conversational-AI products** — names that state the product category outright.
- **Bot-as-a-service and automation platforms** — tools that build or host bots for other businesses.
- **AI agent projects** — a natural extension-level match for the current wave of autonomous AI agents.
- **Developer and API tooling** — projects adjacent to bot infrastructure (webhooks, integrations, orchestration).
- **Short brandable names** — since the namespace was effectively frozen for over five years, many short, desirable strings only became available after October 2023.

**Who it's not ideal for:** a general-purpose company brand unrelated to bots or automation, and any project where the literal word "bot" undercuts trust (e.g., a service trying to convince users a human is involved).

## Notable sites using .bot

.bot spent most of its life as a small, gated namespace, so it has not yet accumulated the flagship public examples older gTLDs have. Its visible footprint since the October 2023 opening skews toward chatbot-platform landing pages, developer-tool projects, and short speculative registrations rather than major consumer brands. Rather than name a site that may not persist, the honest characterization is that .bot's real-world usage is still building out following its recent shift to open registration.

Pick [.ai](/en/tld/ai) when your brand is about artificial intelligence broadly; pick [.app](/en/tld/app) for a software product without a bot-specific angle; pick .bot when the product genuinely is a bot, chatbot, or autonomous agent and you want the domain to say so directly.

## Why choose .bot?

- **Unambiguous category signal.** Few extensions state a product's function as directly as .bot does for anything bot- or agent-related.
- **Newly open, still under-registered.** Because eligibility restrictions only lifted in October 2023, desirable short names are more available than in extensions that have been open for a decade.
- **AI-era timing.** The extension's relaunch coincides with the current wave of chatbot and AI-agent products, so the meaning lands immediately with today's audience.
- **Backed by Amazon Registry Services**, giving it operational stability uncommon among smaller new gTLD registries.

## Things to consider

- **Recent history of restriction.** Some registrars, resellers, and buyers may not yet know the eligibility gate was removed; expect to occasionally explain that registration is now open.
- **Narrow branding fit.** The literal "bot" meaning is a strong asset for bot/agent products and a poor fit for anything else.
- **Still building public recognition.** Because it was gated for years, .bot has not yet built the mainstream familiarity that older, always-open gTLDs have.
- **Two-letter-code caution.** The registry's own policy requires registrants of two-letter-code names to avoid implying government or country-code affiliation — a narrow but real naming restriction.

## Who can register a .bot domain?

**Registration restrictions: open to all (as of October 2023).** From its 2017 launch, .bot required proof that the registrant operated a functioning bot or chatbot. Amazon removed that gate in October 2023, and the current [.bot Registration Policy](https://m.media-amazon.com/images/G/01/arsi/documents/2024.06.20_.BOT_GA_Registration_Policy_-_FINAL.pdf) allocates names on a **first-come, first-served basis** with no eligibility verification, subject only to standard naming rules (1–63 characters, no leading/trailing hyphen) and the registry's Reserved Names, Blocked Names, and Premium Names lists.

The extension supports internationalized domain names (IDNs) and [DNSSEC](/en/glossary/dnssec), and [WHOIS privacy](/en/glossary/whois-privacy/) is available through most registrars. Registrants agree to the Uniform Domain Name Dispute Resolution Policy (UDRP) and Uniform Rapid Suspension (URS) system as part of registration. The binding contractual rules are set out in the [ICANN Registry Agreement for .bot](https://www.icann.org/en/registry-agreements/details/bot).

## .bot pricing and value

.bot pricing dynamics follow the standard new-gTLD pattern: **first-year and renewal pricing typically differ**, so budget for the ongoing renewal rate rather than an introductory offer. The registry reserves a set of **Premium Names** for later release at its own discretion, which can carry materially higher registration and renewal costs than standard names. Because the namespace was effectively frozen for over five years, many short and dictionary-style strings only became registrable after October 2023, which affects which names you'll find already taken versus available. Length, desirability, premium classification, and registrar margin are the main cost drivers; we list no figures here — check current rates at the point of purchase.

## Reputation and email deliverability

.bot does not carry a documented history of spam abuse comparable to some ultra-low-cost, unrestricted-since-launch gTLDs — its multi-year eligibility gate likely limited the bulk, disposable-registration pattern that drives abuse scrutiny elsewhere. That history is short, though: open registration only began in October 2023, so long-term reputation data is still accumulating. Standard **SPF, DKIM, and DMARC** configuration remains good practice for any .bot domain used for email, particularly for automation products where recipients may already be cautious about automated senders.

## Branding and naming tips

- **Let the word do the work.** `.bot` after a product or brand name (`support.bot`, `sales.bot`) states the category without extra copy.
- **Avoid implying a human when there isn't one.** Given rising scrutiny of AI disclosure, a `.bot` domain can actually help set honest expectations rather than obscure them.
- **Watch the two-letter-code rule.** Registering a two-character country-code-style name under .bot requires you not to imply government or ccTLD-manager affiliation.
- **Check for legacy squatters.** Because the namespace was frozen for years, some short strings registered speculatively during the restricted era may already be held — verify availability early.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can anyone register a .bot domain?

Yes, as of the current registry policy. .bot launched in 2017 requiring proof that you actually operated a bot, but Amazon Registry Services removed that restriction in October 2023. Registration is now first-come, first-served with no eligibility verification.

### Does a .bot domain affect SEO?

No. Google treats .bot as a generic top-level domain with no geographic bias and no built-in ranking boost or penalty, the same as any other gTLD.

### Who should register a .bot domain?

Chatbot and conversational-AI products, bot-as-a-service platforms, AI agent projects, automation tools, and developers who want a domain that states outright what the product does. It is a weaker fit for a general-purpose company brand.

### Was .bot always open to everyone?

No. From its 2017 launch until October 2023, Amazon required registrants to demonstrate they operated a functioning bot or chatbot service, which kept the namespace small. Amazon lifted that requirement in October 2023, and the .bot zone reportedly grew roughly 50% within a day.
