The **.able** domain belongs to **Able Inc.**, a Japanese real-estate and apartment-rental company. Unlike a genuine dot-brand TLD, `.able`'s registry agreement carries **no formal ICANN restriction** — but in practice, no registrar sells it and almost no one has ever registered a name under it, so it functions as unavailable regardless. This page explains what `.able` actually is, why it's practically unreachable despite not being legally closed, and what to register instead.

If you searched for the ".able domain" hoping to buy one, the short answer is that no registrar currently offers it — but this page explains exactly what `.able` is, who controls it, why it isn't the open "domain hack" extension it looks like, and what you can register instead.

## What is .able?

`.able` is a **[generic top-level domain](/en/glossary/gtld/)** that ICANN delegated to **Able Inc.**, a real-estate and apartment-rental company based in Japan. The string is a common English adjective and suffix — it's easy to assume `.able` is an open extension built for wordplay domains, and unlike some similar-looking TLDs, it technically is: nothing in the contract limits registration to Able Inc.

That said, `.able` is **not** a dot-brand in the formal ICANN sense. Its [registry agreement with ICANN](https://www.icann.org/en/registry-agreements/details/able) is a standard, non-restricted base agreement — it contains no Specification 13, the clause that would formally designate it a single-registrant brand TLD. What actually keeps `.able` out of reach is simpler and less official: no registrar has ever brought it to market for the public, and [industry tracking shows only a handful of names ever registered](https://ntldstats.com/tld/able), evidently all through the operator's own channels. You can confirm the delegation and operator on the authoritative [IANA root-zone record for .able](https://www.iana.org/domains/root/db/able.html).

## History of .able

The `.able` string was **delegated to the DNS [root zone](/en/glossary/root-zone/) on March 3, 2016**, with **GoDaddy Registry** handling the technical operation on Able Inc.'s behalf. Despite the near-total absence of registrations, the underlying agreement was never a formal brand designation — it's the same type of base agreement used by open, publicly-sold new gTLDs.

Able Inc. runs its main public presence from [able.co.jp](https://www.able.co.jp), and the `.able` extension exists as a largely unused, supplementary namespace the company has never opened up commercially.

## Is .able available to register?

**In practice, no** — though not because ICANN's rules forbid it. No [registrar](/en/glossary/registrar/) — including Namefi — currently lists `.able` for sale, however catchy the word combination. That's a market reality, not a contractual one: the registry agreement itself imposes no eligibility gate, but Able Inc. has never opened the extension to consumer registrars or public marketing, so there's no ordinary channel through which to buy one today.

If you have a genuine business relationship with Able Inc. and a legitimate need for a `.able` address, that request would go through the company's own channels, not a commercial registrar.

## How Able Inc. uses .able

A private, largely-unmarketed extension like `.able` gives a company a space to organize its web presence, even without any contractual restriction forcing it to stay that way. Typical uses seen for a namespace like `.able` include:

- **Property and listing pages** on clean, memorable addresses tied directly to the brand.
- **Campaign and regional pages** that are obviously official because only the company can create them.
- **Internal tools and portals** where a branded suffix signals a trusted, first-party destination.

**Who it's not for:** anyone outside Able Inc. — including the many businesses that might want a *reli.able* or *depend.able* domain hack. Because no registrar carries the extension, there is no practical channel for an independent developer, investor, or business to build on `.able`, even though nothing in ICANN's rules forbids it.

## Notable sites using .able

There are **no third-party sites** on `.able` — [industry tracking shows only a handful of names ever registered](https://ntldstats.com/tld/able), evidently all through the operator's own channels. Able Inc. continues to run most of its public presence from its long-established `.co.jp` domain and uses `.able` selectively, if at all, rather than as a wholesale replacement. Rather than point to a specific address that may change, the honest description is simple: `.able` sits almost entirely unused.

## Why is .able unreachable if it isn't formally restricted?

Some new gTLDs are genuinely closed **dot-brand** TLDs, secured through ICANN's [New gTLD Program](/en/glossary/new-gtld/) with a **Specification 13** designation attached to the registry agreement — a formal clause that limits registration to the trademark owner and its affiliates. `.able` is not one of these: its agreement carries no Specification 13, so ICANN itself treats it as an ordinary, unrestricted registry.

What keeps `.able` unreachable is a market decision, not a contractual one — Able Inc. has simply never brought it to consumer registrars or opened it for public registration. That distinction matters: a genuine Specification 13 dot-brand can *never* be sold to outside registrants under any circumstances, while `.able`'s absence from the market is a choice the operator could reverse. A string looking like a common word does not mean the extension is open in practice — always check whether any registrar actually carries a TLD before planning a domain hack around it.
