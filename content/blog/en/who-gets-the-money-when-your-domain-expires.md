When a [domain](/en/glossary/domain-expiration/) lapses and sells at an expiry [auction](/en/glossary/auction/), the proceeds go to the [registrar](/en/glossary/registrar/). That is the settled arrangement across the industry, and it is stated plainly in the agreements people accept. Porkbun's, effective [17 March 2025](https://porkbun.com/legal/agreement/domain_name_registration_agreement#:~:text=This%20agreement%20is%20effective%20March%2017%2C%202025), tells customers that if it auctions an expired name, ["We do not have to pay You any of the proceeds We may earn as a result of such an auction."](https://porkbun.com/legal/agreement/domain_name_registration_agreement#:~:text=We%20do%20not%20have%20to%20pay%20You%20any%20of%20the%20proceeds%20We%20may%20earn%20as%20a%20result%20of%20such%20an%20auction.)

It is worth remembering that this was not always treated as the obvious answer. In September 2004, two of the industry's most prominent registrars set out to do something more generous: return a share of the sale price to the person who had let the name go. One of them aimed to return most of it.

That effort deserves a better fate than being forgotten. This post is a look back at what was attempted, what actually shipped, the genuinely hard problems it ran into, and the question those problems leave open in 2026. The stage-by-stage mechanics of expiry live in [expired domains and the drop cycle](/en/blog/expired-domains-and-the-drop-cycle/); whether to let a name lapse at all is covered in [when to drop a domain](/en/blog/when-to-drop-a-domain/).

## September 2004: "existing registrants to share in revenue"

On 7 September 2004, Tucows issued a press release headed ["Tucows to Launch Auction Service for Expiring Domain Names"](https://www.tucows.com/news/tucows-to-launch-auction-service-for-expiring-domain-names). Its subheading put the ambition up front: ["Existing Registrants to share in revenue from $60 million market"](https://www.tucows.com/news/tucows-to-launch-auction-service-for-expiring-domain-names#:~:text=Existing%20Registrants%20to%20share%20in%20revenue%20from%20%2460%20million%20market).

Then-CEO Elliot Noss named the problem the service was meant to fix, and named it accurately. ["Presently, most registrants of expiring domain names are unaware of the potential value of their name and do not share in any revenue from the resale of their domain name,"](https://www.tucows.com/news/tucows-to-launch-auction-service-for-expiring-domain-names#:~:text=Presently%2C%20most%20registrants%20of%20expiring%20domain%20names%20are%20unaware%20of%20the%20potential%20value%20of%20their%20name) he said — putting the secondary market for expiring names at roughly $60 million a year, and saying the point of the new service was to let Tucows registrants "get their fair share of this market."

The commitment was unusually concrete for a press release. Tucows said it ["will share all proceeds from the auction service with the former registrant and participating service providers – with the registrant receiving the largest portion."](https://www.tucows.com/news/tucows-to-launch-auction-service-for-expiring-domain-names#:~:text=will%20share%20all%20proceeds%20from%20the%20auction%20service%20with%20the%20former%20registrant%20and%20participating%20service%20providers) And it promised that taking part would cost the owner nothing in rights: the service ["will preserve the original registrant's right to renew or redeem their domain name, with the added bonus of being able to participate in the revenue if the name is resold after it expires."](https://www.tucows.com/news/tucows-to-launch-auction-service-for-expiring-domain-names#:~:text=will%20preserve%20the%20original%20registrant%27s%20right%20to%20renew%20or%20redeem%20their%20domain%20name)

Two weeks later Network Solutions announced a comparable service, and [ICANN](/en/glossary/icann/) put both on the record in an advisory dated [21 September 2004](https://www.icann.org/en/announcements/details/advisory-registrar-expired-name-market-developments-21-9-2004-en). Under both proposals, ICANN wrote, ["the prior registrant would receive a share of the proceeds of the auction"](https://www.icann.org/en/announcements/details/advisory-registrar-expired-name-market-developments-21-9-2004-en#:~:text=the%20prior%20registrant%20would%20receive%20a%20share%20of%20the%20proceeds%20of%20the%20auction). The advisory also recorded how far apart the two were: in Tucows' case ["approximately 80% of the auction price is returned to the prior registrant"](https://www.icann.org/en/announcements/details/advisory-registrar-expired-name-market-developments-21-9-2004-en#:~:text=approximately%2080%25%20of%20the%20auction%20price%20is%20returned%20to%20the%20prior%20registrant), while Network Solutions proposed to distribute 20% or less.

Eighty percent to someone who had already stopped paying is a striking number, then or now.

## An idea that arrived into an unsettled debate

The proposals landed in the middle of a live policy argument, and not a simple one. ICANN's At-Large monthly announcement of [1 October 2004](https://atlarge.icann.org/news/announcement-486-2004-10-01-en) filed the item under the heading ["AUCTIONING EXPIRED DOMAIN NAMES – GOOD OR BAD FOR CONSUMERS?"](https://atlarge.icann.org/news/announcement-486-2004-10-01-en#:~:text=AUCTIONING%20EXPIRED%20DOMAIN%20NAMES) and repeated the figures — ["NSI plans to give 20% or less, Tucows plans to give about 80%"](https://atlarge.icann.org/news/announcement-486-2004-10-01-en#:~:text=NSI%20plans%20to%20give%2020%25%20or%20less%2C%20Tucows%20plans%20to%20give%20about%2080%25).

The community's open questions were not about whether the registrant's cut was large enough. They were prior to that: ["Should registrars have the right to control and auction these names and, if so, what is ICANN's role?"](https://atlarge.icann.org/news/announcement-486-2004-10-01-en#:~:text=Should%20registrars%20have%20the%20right%20to%20control%20and%20auction%20these%20names) The revenue share was attached to a change whose core was that lapsed names would stop [dropping](/en/glossary/pending-delete/) back to the open pool and would instead stay with the registrar to be sold.

This is part of why the idea was harder than it looked. A payout to the former owner was, in the same motion, a defence of a practice much of the community was not yet sold on. The generosity and the controversy travelled together.

## What shipped, and how far it went to keep the promise

The service Tucows described in 2004 as arriving "in late 2004" took longer than planned. By the company's own later account, its in-house auction platform was ["first launched in 2006."](https://www.tucows.com/news/domain-auction-ethics-the-tucows-response#:~:text=These%20rules%20have%20been%20in%20place%20since%20the%20auction%20service%20was%20first%20launched%20in%202006.)

What that platform did carry through is remarkable, and it is the part of this story most worth preserving. Names sold in the in-house auction were not handed straight to the winner. They ["were, in fact, placed on hold for 60 days (referred to as the 'Escrow period') so that if the Original Registrant wanted to reinstate the domain name they would have the ability to do that."](https://www.tucows.com/news/domain-auction-ethics-the-tucows-response#:~:text=were%2C%20in%20fact%2C%20placed%20on%20hold%20for%2060%20days) Tucows stated the consequence itself: ["In short, that means the Original Registrant could reclaim the domain even after it sold at auction."](https://www.tucows.com/news/domain-auction-ethics-the-tucows-response#:~:text=In%20short%2C%20that%20means%20the%20Original%20Registrant%20could%20reclaim%20the%20domain%20even%20after%20it%20sold%20at%20auction.)

The platform's own documentation, quoted in that post, put the window at 100 days from expiry and spelled out who yielded: ["The original registrant can redeem the domain during the escrow period. If the original owner redeems the domain, the winner of the auction will not be granted the domain, but will be refunded the bid amount paid."](https://www.tucows.com/news/domain-auction-ethics-the-tucows-response#:~:text=The%20original%20registrant%20can%20redeem%20the%20domain%20during%20the%20escrow%20period.)

Set that against the industry's present-day rules and it is hard not to admire. A forgetful owner got a hundred days, and a completed sale did not end their claim. Whatever else can be said about the design, it took the promise seriously.

## The hard part

It is also, on inspection, a difficult thing to operate. The contemporaneous record shows at least three real obstacles, and none of them is a matter of anyone's good faith.

**A reversible sale is a weak product.** If the former owner can undo a completed auction for months, the buyer is not buying a domain; they are buying an option somebody else can cancel. Tucows' June 2008 post exists because that tension became concrete. During a platform transition a renewal script failed, roughly 5,800 names from Tucows' own portfolio expired, about 2,800 were automatically listed, and around 260 drew bids from about 25 bidders. Tucows reclaimed them, acting — as it said, and as the published rules allowed — ["completely within our rights as the Original Registrant, and within the publicly stated, publicly available rules of our in-house auction service."](https://www.tucows.com/news/domain-auction-ethics-the-tucows-response#:~:text=completely%20within%20our%20rights%20as%20the%20Original%20Registrant) The rules worked exactly as written. That is the problem: a protection strong enough to be worth having is strong enough to make auction outcomes provisional.

**The pot is smaller than it sounds.** In a Domain Name Wire Q&A dated [12 June 2008](https://domainnamewire.com/2008/06/12/tucows-and-afternic-deal-qa/), Tucows' Bill Sweetman offered a detail that quietly resizes the whole idea: ["only a small percentage of expired names are ever sold at auction."](https://domainnamewire.com/2008/06/12/tucows-and-afternic-deal-qa/#:~:text=only%20a%20small%20percentage%20of%20expired%20names%20are%20ever%20sold%20at%20auction) A revenue share on a rare event is a rare payout. For most owners of most lapsed names, 80% of nothing is nothing — while the cost of building, explaining and administering the scheme is incurred on every name.

**Someone has already paid the renewal.** Registrars are charged by the [registry](/en/glossary/registry/) when a name auto-renews at expiry, whether or not the customer ever pays. Sav's registration agreement states this rationale directly, noting it "is obligated to pay the Registry for all domain renewals on the day the domain name expires" and that ["We therefore take ownership of expired domains until such time as they are renewed, auctioned, sold, or deleted"](https://www.sav.com/terms/domain_name_registration#:~:text=We%20therefore%20take%20ownership%20of%20expired%20domains%20until%20such%20time%20as%20they%20are%20renewed%2C%20auctioned%2C%20sold%2C%20or%20deleted). There is a genuine cost basis sitting under the sale before any split is calculated.

Add the ordinary friction of paying money to someone who has stopped being a customer — a live account, identity and tax handling, a payout rail — and a well-intentioned 80% becomes a demanding thing to run.

## What replaced it

In the same June 2008 post, Tucows announced it was ["working towards the final shut down of this service"](https://www.tucows.com/news/domain-auction-ethics-the-tucows-response#:~:text=working%20towards%20the%20final%20shut%20down%20of%20this%20service) because it had teamed with Afternic for expired-name auctions as of 12 June. In the Q&A published the same week, the interviewer described the new arrangement's split as one where ["resellers who initially sold the domain registration will get 10% of the expired domain selling price"](https://domainnamewire.com/2008/06/12/tucows-and-afternic-deal-qa/#:~:text=resellers%20who%20initially%20sold%20the%20domain%20registration%20will%20get%2010%25%20of%20the%20expired%20domain%20selling%20price). The registrant does not appear in that exchange. The reseller does.

From there, expired names became ordinary inventory. In October 2008 the trade blog TheDomains reported the launch of YummyNames, ["a new service providing exclusive access to a large selection of premium domain names from the Tucows Domain Name Portfolio"](https://www.thedomains.com/2008/10/29/tucows-now-selling-the-expired-domains-they-kept-from-their-customers/#:~:text=a%20new%20service%20providing%20exclusive%20access%20to%20a%20large%20selection%20of%20premium%20domain%20names) — that post is sharply critical of the practice, and the criticism is the blog's own view rather than a documented finding. By [30 September 2016](https://domainnamewire.com/2016/09/30/major-shakeup-tucows-sending-expired-domain-names-godaddy-auctions/), Domain Name Wire reported that ["GoDaddy Auctions has begun auctioning off Tucows' expired domain name inventory"](https://domainnamewire.com/2016/09/30/major-shakeup-tucows-sending-expired-domain-names-godaddy-auctions/#:~:text=GoDaddy%20Auctions%20has%20begun%20auctioning%20off%20Tucows%E2%80%99%20expired%20domain%20name%20inventory), the first time GoDaddy had sold another registrar's expired feed.

## Where the rules stand today

Tucows' current [OpenSRS Master Services Agreement](https://assets.opensrs.com/Uploads/Master_Services_Agreement.pdf) states, in the registrant agreement at Appendix B (PDF page 23), that "Registrant acknowledges and agrees that Registrant's right and interest in a domain name ceases upon its expiration," and that on expiry Tucows "may, at its discretion, elect to assume the registration and may hold it in its own account, delete it, or sell it to a third party." Section 9, "Auction Services" (PDF page 9), reserves to Tucows "the exclusive right to offer the registration to other Users and Registrants" after expiry. Across both of its clauses, Section 9 contains no provision for sharing proceeds with anyone.

The 2006 protection is gone as well, and the boundary moved further than the sale. OpenSRS' current support documentation states that ["Once a domain name enters auction, it can no longer be redeemed by the original registrant."](https://support.opensrs.com/support/solutions/articles/201000063477-domain-name-auctions#:~:text=Once%20a%20domain%20name%20enters%20auction%2C%20it%20can%20no%20longer%20be%20redeemed%20by%20the%20original%20registrant) In 2006 a completed sale could be unwound for 100 days; today, entering the auction ends the claim, before anyone has bid.

Other registrars publish the same shape of rule. Porkbun's knowledge base says an expired name is renewable ["as long as it has not received a bid at auction"](https://kb.porkbun.com/article/37-what-happens-after-a-domain-expires#:~:text=as%20long%20as%20it%20has%20not%20received%20a%20bid%20at%20auction), and that ["Once a bid is placed on a domain at auction it is removed from your account."](https://kb.porkbun.com/article/37-what-happens-after-a-domain-expires#:~:text=Once%20a%20bid%20is%20placed%20on%20a%20domain%20at%20auction%20it%20is%20removed%20from%20your%20account) GoDaddy's expiration timeline says that at 30 days past expiry, ["If there's an active bid at auction the domain cannot be renewed."](https://www.godaddy.com/help/standard-domain-expiration-timeline-609#:~:text=If%20there%27s%20an%20active%20bid%20at%20auction%20the%20domain%20cannot%20be%20renewed)

One partial descendant of the 2004 idea does survive. Epik's support documentation describes NameLiquidate, where a lapsed name is listed for sale and, when it sells, ["the remaining 91% goes to the original owner as In-Store Credit"](https://www.epik.com/support/life-cycle-of-epik-domains/#:~:text=the%20remaining%2091%25%20goes%20to%20the%20original%20owner%20as%20In-Store%20Credit) after a 9% commission. The mechanism sets the scale: the same page describes it as a reverse auction running 7 days (168 hours) ["with a starting price of $999 that drops hourly until it reaches $9"](https://www.epik.com/support/life-cycle-of-epik-domains/#:~:text=with%20a%20starting%20price%20of%20%24999%20that%20drops%20hourly%20until%20it%20reaches%20%249), and the credit is described as funds "that cannot be cashed out." Two details matter for accuracy: the 9%/91% split appears on a support page rather than in Epik's registration agreement, and that agreement conditions redemption on the name being ["not subject to an expired domain name auction bid"](https://www.epik.com/registration/#:~:text=provided%20that%20your%20domain%20name%20is%20not%20subject%20to%20an%20expired%20domain%20name%20auction%20bid). The in-store-credit form is itself an answer to one of the hard problems above: paying in credit sidesteps the payout rail entirely.

## What the record does not say

One thing should be stated plainly, because it is the part a history like this can most easily invent. Neither Tucows nor Network Solutions appears to have published anything announcing the end of the prior-registrant revenue share, and we did not find such a document. There is no press release closing the loop that the September 2004 release opened.

What the public record supports is a sequence, not a stated cause: a 2004 commitment to most of the proceeds with the redemption right preserved; a 2006 platform that honoured the redemption part so thoroughly a sale could be unwound for 100 days; a 2008 move to a third-party auction partner whose published split named resellers rather than registrants; and a present-day set of agreements in which the registrant's interest ends at expiration and no proceeds are owed. The obstacles described above are visible in the contemporaneous documents, but nobody stated them as *the reason*. Anyone claiming to know precisely why the share was dropped is going past the evidence.

## What ICANN did and did not settle

No policy body resolved the money question. ICANN's [Expired Registration Recovery Policy](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/errp-2013-02-28-en) governs what happens to a lapsed [gTLD](/en/glossary/gtld/) registration: it requires expiration notices, requires that the registrant at expiration be permitted to renew during the specified window, and requires that ["all gTLD registries must offer a Redemption Grace Period"](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/errp-2013-02-28-en#:~:text=all%20gTLD%20registries%20must%20offer%20a%20Redemption%20Grace%20Period) of 30 days after deletion. It also confirms that ["registrars may delete registrations at any time after they expire."](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/errp-2013-02-28-en#:~:text=registrars%20may%20delete%20registrations%20at%20any%20time%20after%20they%20expire) The words "proceeds," "auction," "revenue" and "profit" do not appear anywhere in the policy text.

There is a hinge worth noticing in the [redemption period](/en/glossary/redemption-period/) guarantee: it attaches to deletion. A name sold at auction is transferred, not deleted, so that 30-day window never opens for it. ICANN made the same point in a 2023 post, summarising the obligation as permitting "the renewal once the domain name is expired and the redemption during the 30-day redemption period if a domain is deleted."

ICANN has also marked where its authority stops. In that [June 2023 post](https://www.icann.org/en/blogs/details/icann-support-for-registrants-impacted-by-epik-inc-09-06-2023-en) it advised that for concerns about services outside the Registrar Accreditation Agreement — its example being "transactions related to the sale or purchase of domain names through Epik's auction site" — registrants ["may consider seeking legal advice as to how they might obtain relief."](https://www.icann.org/en/blogs/details/icann-support-for-registrants-impacted-by-epik-inc-09-06-2023-en#:~:text=they%20may%20consider%20seeking%20legal%20advice%20as%20to%20how%20they%20might%20obtain%20relief) And none of this reaches [ccTLDs](/en/glossary/cctld/): the same post states that ["ICANN org does not accredit registrars or set policy for ccTLDs."](https://www.icann.org/en/blogs/details/icann-support-for-registrants-impacted-by-epik-inc-09-06-2023-en#:~:text=ICANN%20org%20does%20not%20accredit%20registrars%20or%20set%20policy%20for%20ccTLDs) For `.ai`, `.io`, `.co` and every other country-code extension, the ERRP notice and redemption guarantees do not apply.

## Could it work in 2026?

That is an open question, and it deserves to be asked honestly rather than answered for effect.

Some of what defeated the 2004 attempt has genuinely changed. Noss's core complaint was that owners were "unaware of the potential value of their name" — and in 2004 there was no rule requiring anyone to tell them a name was even expiring. ICANN adopted the ERRP in [2013](https://www.godaddy.com/help/standard-domain-expiration-timeline-609#:~:text=ICANN%20adopted%20the%20Expired%20Registration%20Recovery%20Policy), and mandatory pre- and post-expiry notices are now baseline. Price discovery is better than it was, aftermarket infrastructure is far more mature, and programmable settlement makes splitting a payment between parties much cheaper than it was in 2006.

Some of it has not changed at all. Most expired names still never sell, so most owners would still receive nothing while the administration is charged against every name. The registrar still owes the registry a renewal fee before any split exists. And the sharpest trade-off is untouched: a redemption right meaningful enough to matter still makes an auction result provisional, and buyers still price that uncertainty in. Epik's in-store credit is a real answer to the payout problem, but it is one that keeps the value inside a single company.

So the fair reading is that the 2004 idea was a good one that ran into real engineering and economics, not a cynical one that was quietly abandoned. No registrar or platform offers a general cash share of expiry proceeds to former owners today — Namefi included. Whether the changed conditions of 2026 are enough to make it work is not something this post can settle, and nothing here should be read as a claim that anyone has solved it. It does seem worth someone asking the question again.

## The Namefi angle

The most useful lesson here is not about auctions. It is that a domain's most consequential moment tends to be the one nobody is watching: a renewal that slips, and then a set of rules the owner accepted years earlier and never read. Every document quoted in this post was public the entire time.

[Namefi](https://namefi.io) works on that part of the problem today — clear expiry visibility and renewal control across a [portfolio](/en/glossary/domain-portfolio/), and tokenized ownership that makes control of a real ICANN domain easier to verify and transfer. Not a share of anyone's auction, and no claim to have answered the question above. Just the unglamorous half: not losing the name in the first place.
