When a [domain](/en/glossary/domain-expiration/) lapses and sells at an expiry [auction](/en/glossary/auction/), the money goes to the [registrar](/en/glossary/registrar/). That is not a controversial statement — it is written into the registration agreements people accept, in plain language. Porkbun's agreement, effective [17 March 2025](https://porkbun.com/legal/agreement/domain_name_registration_agreement#:~:text=This%20agreement%20is%20effective%20March%2017%2C%202025), tells its customers that if it auctions an expired name, ["We do not have to pay You any of the proceeds We may earn as a result of such an auction."](https://porkbun.com/legal/agreement/domain_name_registration_agreement#:~:text=We%20do%20not%20have%20to%20pay%20You%20any%20of%20the%20proceeds%20We%20may%20earn%20as%20a%20result%20of%20such%20an%20auction.)

What is less well known is that the industry once announced the opposite. In September 2004, two of the best-known names in domain registration said they would hand the former owner a share of the auction price — in one case, most of it. This post is about that plan: what was promised, what actually shipped, and how the record shows it disappearing. It is a history, not a how-to. The stage-by-stage mechanics of expiry live in [expired domains and the drop cycle](/en/blog/expired-domains-and-the-drop-cycle/), and the question of whether to let a name lapse at all is covered in [when to drop a domain](/en/blog/when-to-drop-a-domain/).

## September 2004: "existing registrants to share in revenue"

On 7 September 2004, Tucows issued a press release headed ["Tucows to Launch Auction Service for Expiring Domain Names"](https://www.tucows.com/news/tucows-to-launch-auction-service-for-expiring-domain-names). Its subheading stated the pitch directly: ["Existing Registrants to share in revenue from $60 million market"](https://www.tucows.com/news/tucows-to-launch-auction-service-for-expiring-domain-names#:~:text=Existing%20Registrants%20to%20share%20in%20revenue%20from%20%2460%20million%20market).

Then-CEO Elliot Noss framed the existing market as one that quietly cut the owner out. ["Presently, most registrants of expiring domain names are unaware of the potential value of their name and do not share in any revenue from the resale of their domain name,"](https://www.tucows.com/news/tucows-to-launch-auction-service-for-expiring-domain-names#:~:text=Presently%2C%20most%20registrants%20of%20expiring%20domain%20names%20are%20unaware%20of%20the%20potential%20value%20of%20their%20name) he said, putting the secondary market for expiring names at about $60 million a year and saying the new service would let Tucows registrants "get their fair share of this market."

The commitment in the release was not hedged. Tucows said it ["will share all proceeds from the auction service with the former registrant and participating service providers – with the registrant receiving the largest portion."](https://www.tucows.com/news/tucows-to-launch-auction-service-for-expiring-domain-names#:~:text=will%20share%20all%20proceeds%20from%20the%20auction%20service%20with%20the%20former%20registrant%20and%20participating%20service%20providers) It also promised that selling the name would not cost the owner the chance to get it back: the service ["will preserve the original registrant's right to renew or redeem their domain name, with the added bonus of being able to participate in the revenue if the name is resold after it expires."](https://www.tucows.com/news/tucows-to-launch-auction-service-for-expiring-domain-names#:~:text=will%20preserve%20the%20original%20registrant%27s%20right%20to%20renew%20or%20redeem%20their%20domain%20name)

Two weeks later, Network Solutions announced a comparable service. [ICANN](/en/glossary/icann/) put both on the record in an advisory dated [21 September 2004](https://www.icann.org/en/announcements/details/advisory-registrar-expired-name-market-developments-21-9-2004-en), noting that under both proposals ["the prior registrant would receive a share of the proceeds of the auction"](https://www.icann.org/en/announcements/details/advisory-registrar-expired-name-market-developments-21-9-2004-en#:~:text=the%20prior%20registrant%20would%20receive%20a%20share%20of%20the%20proceeds%20of%20the%20auction) — and supplying the numbers that make the two plans so different from each other. In Tucows' case, ICANN wrote, ["approximately 80% of the auction price is returned to the prior registrant"](https://www.icann.org/en/announcements/details/advisory-registrar-expired-name-market-developments-21-9-2004-en#:~:text=approximately%2080%25%20of%20the%20auction%20price%20is%20returned%20to%20the%20prior%20registrant); Network Solutions proposed to distribute 20% or less.

## ICANN's community asked whether this was good for anyone

The advisory itself was a neutral notice, but it landed in a live policy argument. ICANN's At-Large monthly announcement of [1 October 2004](https://atlarge.icann.org/news/announcement-486-2004-10-01-en) put the item under the heading ["AUCTIONING EXPIRED DOMAIN NAMES – GOOD OR BAD FOR CONSUMERS?"](https://atlarge.icann.org/news/announcement-486-2004-10-01-en#:~:text=AUCTIONING%20EXPIRED%20DOMAIN%20NAMES) and repeated the split figures — ["NSI plans to give 20% or less, Tucows plans to give about 80%"](https://atlarge.icann.org/news/announcement-486-2004-10-01-en#:~:text=NSI%20plans%20to%20give%2020%25%20or%20less%2C%20Tucows%20plans%20to%20give%20about%2080%25).

It is worth noticing what the community was actually debating. The open questions were not about the size of the registrant's cut. They were more fundamental: ["Should registrars have the right to control and auction these names and, if so, what is ICANN's role?"](https://atlarge.icann.org/news/announcement-486-2004-10-01-en#:~:text=Should%20registrars%20have%20the%20right%20to%20control%20and%20auction%20these%20names) The revenue share was the sweetener on a change whose core was that lapsed names would stop [dropping](/en/glossary/pending-delete/) back to the open pool and would instead stay at the registrar to be sold.

## What actually shipped, and the rule that made it real

The service Tucows described in 2004 as arriving "in late 2004" took longer. By Tucows' own later account, its in-house auction platform was ["first launched in 2006."](https://www.tucows.com/news/domain-auction-ethics-the-tucows-response#:~:text=These%20rules%20have%20been%20in%20place%20since%20the%20auction%20service%20was%20first%20launched%20in%202006.)

What that platform did carry forward from the 2004 promise was the protection for the original owner — and it went remarkably far. In a June 2008 post, Tucows explained that names sold in its in-house auction were not handed straight to the winner. They ["were, in fact, placed on hold for 60 days (referred to as the 'Escrow period') so that if the Original Registrant wanted to reinstate the domain name they would have the ability to do that."](https://www.tucows.com/news/domain-auction-ethics-the-tucows-response#:~:text=were%2C%20in%20fact%2C%20placed%20on%20hold%20for%2060%20days) Tucows stated the consequence itself: ["In short, that means the Original Registrant could reclaim the domain even after it sold at auction."](https://www.tucows.com/news/domain-auction-ethics-the-tucows-response#:~:text=In%20short%2C%20that%20means%20the%20Original%20Registrant%20could%20reclaim%20the%20domain%20even%20after%20it%20sold%20at%20auction.)

The post quoted the platform's own documentation, which put the window at 100 days from expiry and spelled out who lost: ["The original registrant can redeem the domain during the escrow period. If the original owner redeems the domain, the winner of the auction will not be granted the domain, but will be refunded the bid amount paid."](https://www.tucows.com/news/domain-auction-ethics-the-tucows-response#:~:text=The%20original%20registrant%20can%20redeem%20the%20domain%20during%20the%20escrow%20period.)

That is the high-water mark for the expiring owner. A sale was not final against them. Money had changed hands, and the former [registrant](/en/glossary/registrant/) could still undo it.

## June 2008: the in-house auction closes

The same 2008 post that documented those rules also announced their end. Tucows wrote that it was ["working towards the final shut down of this service right now because, as of June 12, we've teamed with Afternic for expired domain name auction services."](https://www.tucows.com/news/domain-auction-ethics-the-tucows-response#:~:text=working%20towards%20the%20final%20shut%20down%20of%20this%20service)

The post exists because the in-house auction had generated a dispute, and the dispute is a clean illustration of the strain in the design. During the technical transition, Tucows says a renewal script failed and roughly 5,800 names from its own portfolio expired; about 2,800 were automatically listed in the auction, and around 260 of those received bids from about 25 bidders. Tucows then reclaimed them — exercising, as it put it, rights that were ["completely within our rights as the Original Registrant, and within the publicly stated, publicly available rules of our in-house auction service."](https://www.tucows.com/news/domain-auction-ethics-the-tucows-response#:~:text=completely%20within%20our%20rights%20as%20the%20Original%20Registrant) The bidders lost the names and got refunds.

Tucows was, in that episode, the original registrant of its own lapsed names, and it said the rules were public and correctly applied. But the incident shows what a redemption right that survives a sale does to the other side of the market: it makes a won auction provisional for months. That is a structural cost of the registrant-friendly design, visible in the contemporaneous record.

When the replacement arrangement was described that same month, the published economics named a different beneficiary. In a Domain Name Wire Q&A dated [12 June 2008](https://domainnamewire.com/2008/06/12/tucows-and-afternic-deal-qa/), the interviewer put it to Tucows' Bill Sweetman that ["resellers who initially sold the domain registration will get 10% of the expired domain selling price"](https://domainnamewire.com/2008/06/12/tucows-and-afternic-deal-qa/#:~:text=resellers%20who%20initially%20sold%20the%20domain%20registration%20will%20get%2010%25%20of%20the%20expired%20domain%20selling%20price) and asked whether that gave resellers a reason to soft-pedal renewal notices. Sweetman answered the incentive question — and, in passing, offered a detail that explains why the whole revenue-share idea was worth less than it sounded: ["only a small percentage of expired names are ever sold at auction."](https://domainnamewire.com/2008/06/12/tucows-and-afternic-deal-qa/#:~:text=only%20a%20small%20percentage%20of%20expired%20names%20are%20ever%20sold%20at%20auction) The registrant is not mentioned in that exchange. The reseller cut is.

## Where the inventory went next

The rest of the arc is a story about expired names becoming ordinary inventory. In October 2008, the trade blog TheDomains reported that Tucows had launched YummyNames to sell names from the Tucows portfolio, describing it as ["a new service providing exclusive access to a large selection of premium domain names from the Tucows Domain Name Portfolio"](https://www.thedomains.com/2008/10/29/tucows-now-selling-the-expired-domains-they-kept-from-their-customers/#:~:text=a%20new%20service%20providing%20exclusive%20access%20to%20a%20large%20selection%20of%20premium%20domain%20names) — and arguing, in the same post, that the practice ought to be stopped. That characterization is the blog's, not Tucows'; the launch itself is the durable fact.

By 2016 the expiring inventory had moved again. Domain Name Wire reported on [30 September 2016](https://domainnamewire.com/2016/09/30/major-shakeup-tucows-sending-expired-domain-names-godaddy-auctions/) that ["GoDaddy Auctions has begun auctioning off Tucows' expired domain name inventory"](https://domainnamewire.com/2016/09/30/major-shakeup-tucows-sending-expired-domain-names-godaddy-auctions/#:~:text=GoDaddy%20Auctions%20has%20begun%20auctioning%20off%20Tucows%E2%80%99%20expired%20domain%20name%20inventory), the first time GoDaddy had sold another registrar's expired feed. The names had gone from "your asset, and you get most of the proceeds" to a wholesale feed sold between platforms.

## What the contracts say now

Read today's documents against the 2004 release and the change is unambiguous.

Tucows' current [OpenSRS Master Services Agreement](https://assets.opensrs.com/Uploads/Master_Services_Agreement.pdf) states, in the registrant agreement at Appendix B (PDF page 23), that "Registrant acknowledges and agrees that Registrant's right and interest in a domain name ceases upon its expiration," and that on expiry Tucows "may, at its discretion, elect to assume the registration and may hold it in its own account, delete it, or sell it to a third party." The same appendix notes that the placeholder page a visitor sees "may feature advertisements posted by Tucows for its own account." Section 9, "Auction Services" (PDF page 9), reserves to Tucows "the exclusive right to offer the registration to other Users and Registrants" after expiry. Across both of its clauses, Section 9 contains no provision for sharing proceeds with anyone.

The protection that defined the 2006 platform is gone too, and it went further than ending at the sale. OpenSRS' current support documentation states flatly that ["Once a domain name enters auction, it can no longer be redeemed by the original registrant."](https://support.opensrs.com/support/solutions/articles/201000063477-domain-name-auctions#:~:text=Once%20a%20domain%20name%20enters%20auction%2C%20it%20can%20no%20longer%20be%20redeemed%20by%20the%20original%20registrant) In 2006 a completed sale could be reversed by the former owner for 100 days. Today, at the same company, entering the auction ends the right — before anyone has bid, let alone paid.

Other registrars publish the same conclusion in their own words. Porkbun's knowledge base says an expired name can be renewed ["as long as it has not received a bid at auction"](https://kb.porkbun.com/article/37-what-happens-after-a-domain-expires#:~:text=as%20long%20as%20it%20has%20not%20received%20a%20bid%20at%20auction), and that ["Once a bid is placed on a domain at auction it is removed from your account."](https://kb.porkbun.com/article/37-what-happens-after-a-domain-expires#:~:text=Once%20a%20bid%20is%20placed%20on%20a%20domain%20at%20auction%20it%20is%20removed%20from%20your%20account) GoDaddy's published expiration timeline says that at 30 days past expiry, ["If there's an active bid at auction the domain cannot be renewed."](https://www.godaddy.com/help/standard-domain-expiration-timeline-609#:~:text=If%20there%27s%20an%20active%20bid%20at%20auction%20the%20domain%20cannot%20be%20renewed) Sav's registration agreement states the ownership position outright: ["We therefore take ownership of expired domains until such time as they are renewed, auctioned, sold, or deleted"](https://www.sav.com/terms/domain_name_registration#:~:text=We%20therefore%20take%20ownership%20of%20expired%20domains%20until%20such%20time%20as%20they%20are%20renewed%2C%20auctioned%2C%20sold%2C%20or%20deleted). And Porkbun's agreement covers the [parking](/en/glossary/domain-parking/) revenue separately from the auction revenue, with the same answer both times: ["We do not have to pay You any of the proceeds We may earn as a result."](https://porkbun.com/legal/agreement/domain_name_registration_agreement#:~:text=We%20do%20not%20have%20to%20pay%20You%20any%20of%20the%20proceeds%20We%20may%20earn%20as%20a%20result.)

## What the record does not say

One thing should be stated plainly, because it is the part a history of this subject can most easily fake. Neither Tucows nor Network Solutions appears to have published an announcement ending the prior-registrant revenue share, and we did not find one. There is no press release closing the loop that the September 2004 release opened.

What the public record supports is a sequence, not a stated cause: a 2004 promise of most of the proceeds and a preserved right to redeem; a 2006 platform that honored the redemption part so strongly a sale could be unwound for 100 days; a 2008 handover to a third-party auction partner whose published split named resellers rather than registrants; and a present-day set of agreements in which the registrant's interest is declared to end at expiration and no proceeds are owed. Anyone claiming to know precisely why the share was dropped — margin, the friction a reversible sale creates for buyers, the small share of names that ever sell, or something never made public — is going beyond what the documents show.

## What ICANN did and did not lock in

It is tempting to assume a policy body settled this. It did not, on the money question.

ICANN's [Expired Registration Recovery Policy](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/errp-2013-02-28-en) is the consensus policy governing what happens to a lapsed [gTLD](/en/glossary/gtld/) registration. It requires expiration notices, requires that the registrant at expiration be permitted to renew during the specified window, and requires that ["all gTLD registries must offer a Redemption Grace Period"](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/errp-2013-02-28-en#:~:text=all%20gTLD%20registries%20must%20offer%20a%20Redemption%20Grace%20Period) of 30 days following deletion. It also confirms that ["registrars may delete registrations at any time after they expire."](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/errp-2013-02-28-en#:~:text=registrars%20may%20delete%20registrations%20at%20any%20time%20after%20they%20expire) The words "proceeds," "auction," "revenue" and "profit" do not appear anywhere in the policy text.

Note the hinge in that [redemption period](/en/glossary/redemption-period/) guarantee: it attaches to deletion. A name sold at auction is transferred, not deleted, so the 30-day recovery window never opens for it. ICANN made the same point in a 2023 blog post about registrant complaints, summarizing the obligation as permitting "the renewal once the domain name is expired and the redemption during the 30-day redemption period if a domain is deleted."

ICANN has also said where its authority stops. In that [June 2023 post](https://www.icann.org/en/blogs/details/icann-support-for-registrants-impacted-by-epik-inc-09-06-2023-en), it advised that for concerns about services outside the Registrar Accreditation Agreement — giving "transactions related to the sale or purchase of domain names through Epik's auction site" as its example — registrants ["may consider seeking legal advice as to how they might obtain relief."](https://www.icann.org/en/blogs/details/icann-support-for-registrants-impacted-by-epik-inc-09-06-2023-en#:~:text=they%20may%20consider%20seeking%20legal%20advice%20as%20to%20how%20they%20might%20obtain%20relief)

One boundary is worth stating because it is routinely missed: none of this reaches [ccTLDs](/en/glossary/cctld/). In the same post ICANN states that ["ICANN org does not accredit registrars or set policy for ccTLDs."](https://www.icann.org/en/blogs/details/icann-support-for-registrants-impacted-by-epik-inc-09-06-2023-en#:~:text=ICANN%20org%20does%20not%20accredit%20registrars%20or%20set%20policy%20for%20ccTLDs) For `.ai`, `.io`, `.co` and every other country-code extension, the ERRP notice and redemption guarantees do not apply; whatever the registry and registrar contracts say is what governs.

## One registrar that still pays something

The 2004 idea has not vanished entirely. Epik's support documentation describes NameLiquidate, where an unrenewed name is listed for sale and, when it sells, ["the remaining 91% goes to the original owner as In-Store Credit"](https://www.epik.com/support/life-cycle-of-epik-domains/#:~:text=the%20remaining%2091%25%20goes%20to%20the%20original%20owner%20as%20In-Store%20Credit) after a 9% commission.

The mechanism sets the scale. The same page describes NameLiquidate as a reverse auction in which domains are listed for 7 days (168 hours), ["with a starting price of $999 that drops hourly until it reaches $9"](https://www.epik.com/support/life-cycle-of-epik-domains/#:~:text=with%20a%20starting%20price%20of%20%24999%20that%20drops%20hourly%20until%20it%20reaches%20%249). The credit is described as usable toward Epik services and as funds "that cannot be cashed out." Two further details matter for accuracy: the 9%/91% split appears on Epik's support page rather than in its registration agreement, and that agreement conditions the redemption window on the name ["not subject to an expired domain name auction bid"](https://www.epik.com/registration/#:~:text=provided%20that%20your%20domain%20name%20is%20not%20subject%20to%20an%20expired%20domain%20name%20auction%20bid). Readers can do their own arithmetic on the ceiling.

## The Namefi angle

The through-line of this history is that a domain's most expensive moment is the one where nobody is paying attention: an unrenewed name changes hands under rules the owner accepted years earlier and has never read. Every document quoted above was public the whole time.

[Namefi](https://namefi.io) works on the part of that problem that is addressable today — knowing what you hold and when it comes due. Clear expiry visibility and renewal control across a [portfolio](/en/glossary/domain-portfolio/), and tokenized ownership that makes control of a real ICANN domain easier to verify and transfer, are hedges against exactly the failure mode this history is made of: a renewal nobody saw, followed by a set of rules nobody read.
